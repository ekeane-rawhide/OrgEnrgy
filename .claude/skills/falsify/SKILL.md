# The falsification loop

Derived from a session in which **eight** claims were reported as findings
and **eight** were later destroyed — all eight invisible to the author from
the inside, all eight caught from outside. This encodes what caught them.

The loop's purpose is not to be rigorous-feeling. It is to make it possible
for the world to say *no*.

## 0. The precondition that decides everything

**Ask first: can this test return an answer I did not put there?**

A simulation cannot falsify the theory it implements. At best it catches
coding errors. If the data being tested was generated by the equations
being tested, the maximum available yield is *"my algebra is
self-consistent"* — never a fact about the world.

If your data is self-generated, say so explicitly in every claim, and do
not use the words "confirmed", "validated", or "law".

## 1. Pre-register, and pre-register the *kill*

Before running: write the numeric prediction **and** the condition that
would falsify it, in the same breath.

Then check the kill condition itself:
- Is the threshold derived, or guessed? *(Three thresholds in the source
  session were guesses wearing the costume of predictions.)*
- Prefer **threshold-free tests**: fit two competing models and report
  which wins, rather than testing one against a hand-picked bar.

## 2. The vacuity check — run this before trusting any result

**Sweep the mechanism the hypothesis blames. If the verdict doesn't move,
the test is dead.**

Concretely: toggle or sweep the thing you claim is responsible, and watch
the measured quantity. If it doesn't change, that mechanism is inert in
your tested regime and your verdict was fixed by construction.

Real instances this catches:
- A test whose return value was a hard-coded tautology
- A "physical" mechanism provably inert (1e-17) across the entire
  pre-registered window
- A diffusive medium run at a diffusion length of 0.45 cells — numerically
  the zero-diffusion limit
- An impossibility search scoped narrowly enough to always confirm

Writing an anti-vacuity warning does **not** substitute for running this
check. In the source session the warning and the error appeared in the
same file.

## 3. Sweep your own analyst choices

Every number you chose without noticing you were choosing — window width,
sweep range, bin size, threshold, subset — is a free parameter.

**Report the spread across those choices, never a single configuration.**
A result that inverts under an equally defensible window is not a result.
*(One session claim's headline "perfect ordering" reversed entirely under
a different, equally reasonable sweep range — it had never swept its own
sweep.)*

## 4. Distrust perfect agreement

**Machine-precision agreement means identity, not confirmation.**

If a simulated measurement matches a closed-form prediction to ~1e-12,
the two are the same computation. Check whether your "prediction" solves
in closed form the fixed point your "measurement" numerically converges
to. *(This destroyed the source session's strongest claimed law, which
had been defended precisely on its 0.00% error.)*

Related: check what your formula silently hardcodes. State the domain the
claim actually holds in, then test *outside* it. A law advertised as
general was found false at 33% error by varying a constant that was never
mentioned in the claim.

## 5. The baseline must be able to win

A comparator that cannot fight back proves nothing.

- Compare against the **strongest fair alternative**, not a convenient one.
- Always include a **trivial control matched on the relevant average**.
  If a constant policy at your adaptive scheme's own average performance
  beats it, the adaptation is doing nothing.
  *(This inverted a claimed 3.13× advantage into a 22% deficit.)*
- If your baseline is one nobody would actually deploy, you have a
  strawman.

## 6. The adversarial pass — mandatory, and disinterested

Hand the work to a reviewer with **no stake in the outcome** and
instructions to destroy it. The corrective is not intelligence; it is
disinterest. Smaller/cheaper models work fine.

Brief them that all of these count as kills:
- **reduction** — it's known prior art under another name
- **vacuous** — the verdict was fixed by construction
- **rigged** — an artifact of parameter or baseline choice
- **bug**

Hand them your own strongest attack against yourself, not a neutral
summary. The passes that found the most were the ones aimed at the
author's weakest point deliberately.

**Verify the adversary's central claim yourself before accepting it** —
and accept it when it holds.

## 7. Verdict taxonomy — four outcomes, do not collapse them

1. **Survived** — attacked by a named adversary and held. Cite the attack.
2. **True but trivial** — correct, general, bug-free, and a restatement of
   something standard. Not a discovery. This category is essential and
   usually missing.
3. **Unaudited** — believed, never attacked. Never list alongside (1).
4. **Killed** — with the reason.

Also mark: **known rediscovery** (true, real, already named elsewhere).

## 8. Stopping rules

- Two failed repairs on one claim → **halt**. A third repair is selected
  knowing the target and carries no evidential weight.
- Repair the *derivation*, never the formula. A patch fitted to the datum
  that falsified you is worthless.
- Fresh test points must exercise a **different mechanism**, not re-run
  the same one — otherwise they cannot distinguish a general law from the
  closed-form solution of your one toy model.

## 9. Report

State plainly: what survived attack, what is merely unaudited, what is
true-but-known, what died. If nothing survived, say that. A clean
falsification is a result; an unfalsifiable claim is not.
